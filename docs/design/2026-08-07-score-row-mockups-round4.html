Home screen · score row · round four

Four equal cells was never a requirement — it was an assumption.

Three rounds, sixty-six mockups, and every single one kept the same skeleton: four lanes, same width, same weight, same treatment. That's the thing Oura and Garmin both refuse to do. Oura's home is one score. Everything else on that screen is context for it. If this row is the first thing you see and it matters, then the strongest minimal move left isn't another way to draw a number — it's deciding that one of the four is more important than the other three.

Readiness is the hero

It's the only one of the four that answers a question you act on. Heart rate, sleep and activity are how it got there. Family N makes that structural instead of leaving it for you to reconstruct every morning.

Thin at scale

Weight 200–300 numerals are the most recognisable thing about Oura's interface, and they only work large. A hero row is the one place in this app that has the size to carry them.

Units answer "52 what?"

Garmin labels every figure. O3 and P3 are the only designs in four rounds that add information without adding a graphic — and they close a gap the row has always had.

The one real cost of the hierarchy designs

N1, N3 and N4 give Readiness a much bigger tap target than the other three — and the three secondary cells land at roughly 90×46dp, which is under the 48dp floor. They'd need their padding opened up before shipping, which costs back some of the compactness that makes them appealing. N2 Weighted line is the one that doesn't have this problem: every cell stays full height, only the type scale changes.

The second cost is subtler. Making Readiness the hero is a claim that it's the most useful of the four every day. On a morning after a bad night, the sleep score is the number you want. A fixed hierarchy can't know that — and a hierarchy that reorders itself is no longer minimal.
